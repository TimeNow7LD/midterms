160 years of midterms: the Senate

In the Senate, the president's party lost ground in 27 of 41 midterms since 1858. The penalty is real, but smaller and less predictable than in the House.

Why the Senate swings less than the House

Every House seat is on the ballot every two years. In the Senate, only about one-third of the seats are up in any election, because senators serve six-year terms. So even a big wave can only reach a third of the chamber.

The Senate is also a smaller pizza. With 100 seats today, and as few as 50 in the Civil War era, each seat is a bigger slice. One seat today is 1 point of the Senate. In 1862, with the Southern states gone, one seat was worth about 2 points. That's why Lincoln's party gained just 2 Senate seats in 1862 but 9.4 points of the Senate.

Before 1913, voters didn't pick senators

Until the 17th Amendment was ratified in 1913, U.S. senators were chosen by state legislatures, not by voters directly. So the Senate results before 1914 reflect who won the statehouses, one step removed from the voters.

The exceptions

The president's party gained Senate share 12 times, including 1934 (Franklin Roosevelt, +11.3 points), 1898 (McKinley, +10.7) and 1862 (Lincoln, +9.4). In 1998 and 2022 it essentially broke even. Since 1946, the typical Senate loss has been about 3.5 points.

Sources and method

  • Seat counts: House of Representatives and Senate historical records, as compiled by USAFacts (composition of Congress after each election, 1856–2024).
  • Midterms only: presidential election years are excluded. Andrew Johnson's 1866 midterm is excluded because his party affiliation was ambiguous.
  • Shares count only Democratic and Republican seats; third-party members and vacancies are left out.
  • Senators were chosen by state legislatures until the 17th Amendment (1913).
  • Change is measured from the composition after the previous election to the composition after the midterm.
