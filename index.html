Why we're doing this

We're tracking career politicians and building a campaign reform plan that works for both parties and for the American people. That takes time, research and your support.

In 1958, my father's one manual labor job paid $2.50 an hour: 40 hours a week, every weekend off. It paid for the brand-new two-story brick home he built by hand in Schenectady, New York, with a full basement and a rental apartment upstairs, on a 20-year mortgage. It came with company-paid health care and retirement for a family of five. It kept Mom home raising three young kids, with home-cooked meals and Dad at the other end of the dinner table at 6 o'clock every night. Mom was the homework wizard. Dad was the disciplinarian, following Mom's instructions, because she was home with us all day. A late-model car sat in the garage, and 10% of every paycheck went into savings, because credit cards didn't exist. No government help of any kind.

I remember. I was six, the oldest of three. I believed that world would be even better for the next generation. Instead, it got worse. Much worse. Now we're pulling back the curtain to show you why.

By our estimate, it takes about $80 an hour to afford that same life today. We show the receipts. You decide.

About the $80 estimate: it's our calculation of what one 40-hour-a-week earner needs today for the same life: a brand-new home on a 20-year mortgage, a parent at home raising three kids, and no government benefits. It's not an official statistic. Adjusted only for official inflation, $2.50 in 1958 is roughly $28 today, but the Bureau of Labor Statistics itself says its Consumer Price Index "differs in important ways from a complete cost-of-living measure." Our full math and sources are coming soon.
