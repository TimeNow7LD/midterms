160 years of midterms: history held in 2026

From 1858 through 2022, the president's party lost ground in the House in 37 of 41 midterms, under Democrats and Republicans alike. In 2026 it happened again: President Trump's party lost about 47 seats, by preliminary count.

The House going into 2027

All 435 House seats were on the ballot on November 3, 2026. By the Associated Press count, 48 seats changed party: Democrats flipped 45 and Republicans flipped 3. Ten races are still undecided, including two Louisiana seats headed to a December 12 runoff.

Term length: 2 years, so every seat is on the ballot every election. Term limits: none. Seats per state are set by population after each census; the total has been fixed at 435 since 1913.

Why percentages instead of seats?

A 30-seat loss in 1870 isn't the same as a 30-seat loss today. Back then the House had about 240 members. Today it has 435. The House grew as new states joined and the population climbed, until Congress capped it at 435 in 1913. The Senate grew too, adding two senators with each new state, until it reached 100 in 1959.

So this chart measures each loss as a share of the chamber: how much of Congress the president's party lost, not just how many seats.

The pizza problem: 1902

In 1902, Theodore Roosevelt's Republicans gained 7 House seats, yet their share of the House fell 2.9 points. How? The House grew after the 1900 census.

Think of a pizza. You have 4 of 8 slices: half the pizza. Then the pizza grows to 12 slices and you get 5. You gained a slice, but you went from half the pizza to about 42%. You gained a slice and still lost ground.

The reverse happened in 1878: Rutherford Hayes's Republicans lost 4 seats but gained 1.6 points of the House.

"Points" vs. "percent"

A drop of 2.9 percentage points means the party's share went from 57.0% to 54.0% of the House: 2.9% of the whole chamber. That's what this chart shows.

The exceptions

Only four midterms bucked the pattern in the House: 1878 (Hayes), 1934 (Franklin Roosevelt, during the New Deal), 1998 (Clinton) and 2002 (George W. Bush, after 9/11).

The 2026 bar (preliminary)

History held. Counting called races plus the current leaders in eight uncalled races, President Trump's party lost about 47 House seats in 2026, or about 10.6 points of the House. That's a bigger loss than the typical midterm (about 9 points), though smaller than 2010 (−14.7) or 1994 (−12.4). Two Louisiana seats go to a December 12 runoff, so the final number can still move a little. The bar becomes final once every state certifies its results.

Sources and method

  • Seat counts: House of Representatives and Senate historical records, as compiled by USAFacts (composition of Congress after each election, 1856–2024).
  • Midterms only: presidential election years are excluded. Andrew Johnson's 1866 midterm is excluded because his party affiliation was ambiguous.
  • Shares count only Democratic and Republican seats; third-party members and vacancies are left out.
  • 2027 House: current members from the @unitedstates congress-legislators dataset, updated with the 48 seat changes and 10 undecided races reported by the Associated Press as of November 14, 2026 (via California Today's results guide). The AP compares district numbers, so a few seats in states that redrew their maps are counted as flips by district number. Results are unofficial until certified.
  • The preliminary 2026 bar counts called races plus current leaders in eight uncalled races, and leaves out the two Louisiana runoff seats.
  • Change is measured from the composition after the previous election to the composition after the midterm.
